## What actually happened

In the 1980s Pablo Escobar brought a small group of common hippos (*Hippopotamus amphibius*), usually reported as one male and three females, to the private zoo at Hacienda Nápoles in Antioquia. After his death in 1993 most of the zoo's animals were moved elsewhere. The hippos stayed, because moving them was too difficult.

The climate suited them, there was plenty of water and grazing, there were no droughts and no predators. They began to breed and left the hacienda's ponds for the Magdalena River and its tributaries. A census carried out in 2021–2022 by the National University of Colombia, the Humboldt Institute and the regional authority Cornare estimated **181–215 animals**. That was about twice as many as earlier models predicted. Without management, projections range from several hundred to well over a thousand within a few decades.

## Why it matters ecologically

- **Hippos change the water they live in.** They graze on land at night and defecate in the water by day, so they move huge amounts of nutrients from land into rivers and lakes. Shurin et al. (2020) found more terrestrial carbon in Colombian lakes with hippos. The algae there were dominated by cyanobacteria, and daily oxygen swings were stronger.
- **They compete with native species.** The Antillean manatee, the spectacled caiman, river turtles and capybaras all use the same shallow waters and riverbanks.
- **They fill an empty slot.** South America lost its megafauna about 12,000 years ago, including giant ground sloths, toxodonts and gomphotheres. Lundgren et al. (2020) argue that introduced herbivores like the hippos partly restore ecological roles that have been missing since then. That makes the case scientifically interesting, not just a problem.
- **They are dangerous to people.** Hippos are among the most dangerous large animals in Africa. People fish, farm and live along the Magdalena.

Colombia declared the hippos an invasive species in 2022. Sterilisation, relocation and culling have all been proposed, debated and partly tried. See the sources for the latest status.

## Where this site begins

This site does not argue for or against control. It asks a different question:

> **What if the hippos stay, and evolution gets twenty million years to work on them and their neighbours?**

Each era below is written by the editors from real analogues: island dwarfism, aquatic convergence, the giant caimans of the Miocene. Each one leaves **open niches**, gaps in the ecosystem that you can fill with your own species.
